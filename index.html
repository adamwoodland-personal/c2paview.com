Questions and answers

    

      
What are Content Credentials?

      
An open provenance standard from the Coalition for Content Provenance and Authenticity (C2PA), backed by Adobe, Google, Microsoft, OpenAI, Leica, Nikon, the BBC and others. A camera or app cryptographically signs what it produced, the signature covers the file's actual content, and every later edit can be recorded and verified. AI generators use it to label their output; news agencies use it to prove a photo came from the camera that shot it. More: what C2PA is and who runs it.

    

    

      
How do I check if an image is AI-generated?

      
Drop it in above. If the generator added Content Credentials (ChatGPT, Adobe Firefly, Google's Nano Banana Pro and others do), the actions list will say so - this page flags entries whose digital source type is trained algorithmic media as AI-generated. Absence of credentials does not prove a file is not AI-made: metadata is easily lost along the way, and OpenAI notes it "can be stripped, lost through uploads and downloads, or broken by transformations like file format changes, resizing, or screenshots". Step by step: how to check if an image is AI-generated.

    

    

      
Can this detect a SynthID watermark?

      
No. SynthID is an invisible watermark from Google DeepMind, woven into the image, video or sound itself rather than stored as metadata. Google's SynthID Detector at synthid.com checks for it in files from Google's AI and from partners including OpenAI; the Gemini app also checks for Google's, and openai.com/verify for OpenAI's. This page reads C2PA Content Credentials, the signed record of who made a file and how. The two often arrive together: images made with ChatGPT since May 2026 carry both, and so do images made with Nano Banana Pro in the Gemini app.

    

    

      
What does a green result prove?

      
That the file carries Content Credentials, the cryptographic checks pass, the file has not been modified since signing, and the signer's certificate chains to the C2PA trust list or the older interim trust list. If the file is intact but the signer is not on those lists, or the lists could not be loaded, an amber result says so - the identity of the signer can't be verified. A red result means a check failed, and it says which: content that no longer matches means the file was changed after signing, while a bad signature, certificate or manifest means the credentials can't be relied on, which by itself doesn't show the file was edited. What each code means: C2PA validation codes explained.

    

    

      
What does 'Who vouches for it' mean?

      
Some files also carry an identity assertion, defined by the Creator Assertions Working Group (CAWG): a named person or organisation signs to vouch for parts of the manifest, such as the content or the AI training preferences, either with an identity certificate or through an identity provider that has verified their accounts. This page shows who is named and what they vouch for, and checks the claim's signature separately from the file's own verdict. No trust list for identity credentials is set up here, so the person or organisation named is always shown as unconfirmed, never as verified.

    

    

      
Why does my file have no Content Credentials?

      
That is the normal case. Only recent cameras and apps sign their output, and many websites and messaging apps strip metadata on upload - a signed photo posted to social media can come back out unsigned. No credentials means no provenance information either way: it is not evidence of tampering.

    

    

      
Is my file uploaded anywhere?

      
No. Validation is done by the Content Authenticity Initiative's open-source C2PA library (c2pa-rs), compiled to WebAssembly and running in your browser. This site has no backend, no accounts, no analytics and no cookies; the file's bytes never leave your machine. Load the page, go offline, and it still works.

    

    

      
Is this the official Content Credentials checker?

      
No. The Content Authenticity Initiative has its own Verify tool at verify.contentauthenticity.org. This viewer is independent: it is built on the Content Authenticity Initiative's open-source C2PA library, runs entirely in your browser, and is not affiliated with or endorsed by the C2PA or the Content Authenticity Initiative.

    

    

      
Which file types are supported?

      
JPEG, PNG, WebP, AVIF, GIF, TIFF, SVG, MP4, MOV, MP3, WAV and PDF, plus detached .c2pa sidecar manifest files - the formats the C2PA specification defines embeddings for.

    

    

      
Can Content Credentials be faked or removed?

      
They can be removed - they are metadata, and the C2PA standard itself anticipates redaction - but they cannot be forged or altered without detection, because the signature covers the content and any tampering shows as a validation failure. That asymmetry is the point: credentials that are present and green are strong evidence; missing credentials say nothing.

    

    

      
Why does a file from Claude or another AI assistant have Content Credentials?

      
AI tools increasingly sign what they produce. Anthropic's Claude adds Content Credentials to some file types it creates or modifies, images made with ChatGPT or the OpenAI API carry them, since May 2026 alongside a SynthID watermark, and Adobe adds them to images generated entirely with Firefly. If it is one of the supported file types, drop it above to see who signed it, when, and what it declares about how the file was made.

    

    

      
How do I add Content Credentials to my own files?

      
Use a signing tool. ZeroFake can add C2PA Content Credentials to photos you take using the ZeroFake app; Adobe's Creative Cloud apps sign exports when Content Credentials are enabled; and the C2PA project's open-source c2patool signs from the command line. This site is read-only by design - it inspects credentials but never alters your file.
