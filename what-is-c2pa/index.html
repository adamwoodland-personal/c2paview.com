What is C2PA?

C2PA is an open technical standard for recording where a digital file came from and how it has been changed. The record it defines is called Content Credentials: a signed history carried with the file, so anyone can check who signed it, whether that signer is on a trust list, and whether the file has been altered since.

Updated 22 November 2026

Check a file's Content Credentials

C2PA in one paragraph

The Coalition for Content Provenance and Authenticity, or C2PA, in its own words "provides an open technical standard for publishers, creators and consumers to establish the origin and edits of digital content. It's called Content Credentials". A camera, app or AI tool that supports the standard writes a record into the file and signs it with a certificate. Later, any validator, such as c2paview, can check that signature and whether the file still matches what was signed.

Who is behind it

  • Founded 22 February 2021 by Adobe, Arm, BBC, Intel, Microsoft and Truepic. It brought together the Adobe-led Content Authenticity Initiative and Project Origin, which Microsoft and the BBC led.
  • Today it is "a project of the Joint Development Foundation, a Washington-based 501c6 non-profit". Its steering committee lists Adobe, Amazon, the BBC, Google, Meta, Microsoft, OpenAI, Publicis Groupe, Sony, TikTok and Truepic.
  • The Content Authenticity Initiative (CAI) was "founded by Adobe in 2019, and Adobe continues to lead it". It builds the open-source tools, including c2pa-rs, the library c2paview runs in your browser.
  • Project Origin's work on news provenance now continues at the IPTC.
  • The specification is at version 2.5, dated October 2026. Version 1.0 was announced in January 2022.

What a manifest records

Each set of Content Credentials is a manifest. Its parts:

  • Assertions: statements about the file, such as what was done to it (created, opened, edited, converted), how it was made (for example captured with a camera, or generated by AI), and what it was made from.
  • Hard binding: a hash of the file's content. If the bytes change, the hash no longer matches and the check fails with assertion.dataHash.mismatch or a similar code.
  • The claim: the signed part. It names the app that made the record (the "claim generator") and points to every assertion by its hash, so a changed assertion no longer matches the signed claim and fails with assertion.hashedURI.mismatch.
  • The signature: made with the signer's certificate, sometimes with a trusted time-stamp. The certificate names the signer, such as OpenAI, Google LLC or a camera maker.
  • Ingredients: earlier files this one was made from. Each can carry its own manifest, so a file's history can be a chain of signed steps.

A validator then sorts the manifest into the specification's states: Well-Formed, Valid (unchanged since signing, signature good) and Trusted (also signed by a certificate on a trust list). Each check is reported as a status code: see C2PA validation codes explained.

Trust lists and conformance

A valid signature proves the file is unchanged since someone signed it. Whether you can trust that someone depends on the trust lists. The specification defines the C2PA Trust List as "a C2PA-managed list of X.509 certificate trust anchors that issue certificates to hardware & software signers". Products earn a place through the C2PA Conformance Program, launched on 4 June 2025: "54 products from 31 companies have passed conformance (as of May 14, 2026)". An earlier interim trust list was frozen on 1 January 2026; during the transition, validators may consult both it and the C2PA trust list, but must tell the two apart. c2paview checks the official lists and the frozen interim list, does not yet tell the two apart, and shows amber when the signer is on neither.

The "cr" icon

On 10 October 2023 the C2PA introduced the official Content Credentials icon, "a minimalist pin containing the letters "CR"". The C2PA owns its trademark. Its design guidance says the icon "should already indicate the presence of a valid manifest". LinkedIn, for one, marks signed images and videos with it.

Identity: who vouches for a file

The Creator Assertions Working Group (CAWG) "builds upon the work of the Coalition for Content Provenance and Authenticity (C2PA) by defining additional assertions that allow content creators to express individual and organizational intent about their content". Since March 2025 it has been a working group of the Decentralized Identity Foundation. Its identity assertion (version 1.3, ratified 17 August 2026) lets a named person or organisation sign to vouch for a file. c2paview shows it as "Who vouches for it", always as unconfirmed, because no trust list for identity credentials is set up here.

What C2PA cannot prove

  • That the picture is true. The record shows what the signer declared and, when the signer is on a trust list, who they are. Google's Gemini help puts it plainly: Content Credentials don't tell you "if an image, video, or audio file is definitely AI-generated", but give origin and history to judge it by.
  • Anything, when it is missing. Credentials can be removed. The C2PA's own guidance says "embedded information can be stripped, watermarks can be defeated, and fingerprint matching is probabilistic rather than exact". No credentials means no information either way.
  • An exact time, from a camera's clock. Canon notes that capture times "are recorded based on the camera's internal clock, and are therefore not guaranteed to exactly match the actual date and time of capture". A trusted time-stamp, when present, dates the signature itself.
  • Who an unlisted signer really is. A valid signature from a certificate on no trust list shows the file is unchanged since it was signed, not who signed it.

Questions

Is C2PA the same as Content Credentials?

Nearly. C2PA is the coalition and its technical standard; Content Credentials is the name for the record that standard defines and puts in a file. The C2PA says of its standard: "It's called Content Credentials".

Is C2PA a watermark?

No. Content Credentials are signed metadata carried with the file, and they can be stripped. A watermark such as Google's SynthID is hidden in the image or sound itself. The two can work together: a copy of the record kept online can be found again through a watermark or a fingerprint, which the C2PA calls "Durable Content Credentials".

How do I check a file's Content Credentials?

Drop it into c2paview's viewer, which runs the Content Authenticity Initiative's open-source C2PA library in your browser and never uploads the file. It shows who signed it, what was done, and whether it has changed since. A file shared through social media or a messaging app may have lost its credentials, so use the original.
